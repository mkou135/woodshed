# Taking a solo apart and practising it — research notes (2026-08-23)

What the pedagogy and practice literature says about turning a transcription
into practice. Researched because the owner judged the methodology "could be
even more important" than the analysis. Evidence levels are marked; much of
this field is consensus among teachers rather than controlled study.

## 1. The canonical methods

**Clark Terry — "imitate, assimilate, innovate."** The most-cited framing. The
concrete assimilation procedure (Jazzadvice's gloss): sing the rhythms,
accents and exact pitches first; repeat lines "hundreds of times"; take them
through all keys, ranges and inversions; start slow. A lick in one key "is
not going to cut it."
https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/clark-terrys-3-steps-to-learning-improvisation/
*Slogan plus one site's elaboration; matches Galper and Baker.*

**Berliner, *Thinking in Jazz* (1994), ch. 2 and ch. 4 "Getting Your
Vocabulary Straight".** Ethnography of 50+ professionals. Players sang and
clapped along with records "very precisely by ear" before the instrument,
then checked memorised solos against the original as memory training. Ch. 4
is about learning *models* for solo formulation — vocabulary chunks, not
whole solos. *Strongest evidence for "sing before play" and "the unit is the
phrase".*

**Baker, *How to Play Bebop* vols 1–3 (1985–87).** Vol. 3 is "how to practice
bebop … and what to do with the transcriptions": identify a line's harmonic
function (e.g. starts on the 3rd of the bebop scale), classify by device
(bebop scale, enclosure, 7–3 resolution) and the progression it fits, play
in twelve keys.

**Coker, *Elements of the Jazz Language for the Developing Improvisor*
(1991).** The closest thing to a published taxonomy for dissecting solos.
Chapters: Digital Patterns and Scalar Patterns, 7–3 Resolutions, Harmonic
Generalization, Sequence, CESH (contrapuntal elaboration of static harmony),
Quotes, the "Cry Me a River" lick; index adds change-running, bebop scales,
enclosure, 3-note figures. His own method statement: elements "are isolated
for study: they are examined in recorded solos, suggestions are made for
using each element … and specific exercises are provided" — *locate in a
real solo → explain → drill in several keys → apply to standard
progressions.* This is woodshed's pipeline in book form.

**Crook, *How to Improvise* (1991).** Practice design rather than
transcription: What/How/When to play, and **isolate one variable** — phrase
length, rhythmic density, pacing, dynamics, articulation, play/rest ("rest 1
bar, play 1 bar") — on a daily schedule.

**Galper, "Developing Style, Part 1."** "Copy only those ideas that strike
you on an emotional, gut level … Make them into exercises and learn them in
all twelve keys. Once you've learned them, try to alter them to fit over
other sets of changes. Reorder the components in the idea." And: "Practice
only what you like!" https://halgalper.com/articles/developing-style-part-1/
*The clearest voice for selective extraction and recombination.*

**Bergonzi, *Inside Improvisation* vol. 1.** A drill grammar: 4-note cells,
24 permutations, one per chord through a tune; "one or two permutations
from each column is sufficient."

**Mintzer, *14 Jazz & Funk Etudes*.** Each etude has a short header naming
what to notice, and a practice note (slow first). *UI pattern: "things to
look for" precedes the notes.*

**Online methods.** Chad LB: transpose by ear a half-step at a time, think
in scale degrees and shapes. Bob Reynolds: transcribe "one phrase at a
time" by ear; "Play 3, Sing 1". Skyler Floe (Downbeat Academy) six steps:
find a lick and its changes → analyse and memorise → 12 keys by ear → write
your own variants → apply over tunes with iReal → learn the variants in 12
keys, daily for weeks.
https://downbeatacademy.com/articles/i-transcribed-now-what
https://www.jazzlessonvideos.com/post/how-to-take-a-phrase-through-12-keys

## 2. Consensus procedure (≥3 independent sources)

1. **Listen a lot before touching the horn.**
2. **Sing it** — pitches, rhythm, accents — before playing.
3. **Work in phrases**, not notes or whole solos; loop a phrase slowed down.
4. **Play it with the recording** to copy articulation and time feel.
5. **Analyse**: chord function of each note, scale-degree numbers, the
   device name.
6. **Break into micro-units** — "a measure long, a couple of beats, anything
   you can combine easily" (Jazzadvice, *Why Transcribing a Whole Solo Has
   Gotten You Nowhere*); Galper's "reorder the components".
7. **Transpose through 12 keys by ear**, by half-step or by degree, slow
   then faster.
8. **Take it through a tune** — every ii–V / turnaround / major / minor
   where it fits.
9. **Vary it**: rhythm, start beat, recombine fragments, then write 2–3 of
   your own lines with the same device. (Barry Harris on shifting phrase
   start points.)
10. **Stay with one phrase long enough.**

Single-source, plausible: practise the *rhythm* of a phrase on one note;
"gravity / target note" framing.

## 3. Beyond licks

Widely shared but vague: sound, time, articulation, behind/on/ahead of the
beat, ghost notes, accents, pacing and space, motivic development.

The one empirical source: **Frieler et al. 2016, *Midlevel analysis of
monophonic jazz solos*** (Jazzomat) — about a quarter of units have a
motivic relation to a preceding unit, mean chain length 2.8. *Architecture
is short local motivic chains, not chorus-long development.* Crook's
isolation variables (phrase length, density, play/rest ratio) are the only
operationalised list of non-pitch style markers found. No validated "what
makes it THIS player" checklist exists; that is folklore territory.

## 4. Learning science

- **Quality over quantity.** Duke, Simmons & Cash 2009, *It's Not How Much;
  It's How* (JRME): retention unrelated to time or trial count; the best
  pianists isolated errors, varied tempo systematically, repeated the target
  until fixed. *Strong.*
- **Interleaving.** Stambaugh 2011 (novice clarinet): random order no
  better at end of practice, significantly better at retention. Carter &
  Grahn 2016 (advanced clarinet): interleaved 3-min blocks beat 12-min
  blocks on retention; players *preferred* blocked. *Moderate, small N,
  not jazz.*
- **Experts insert pre-learned chunks.** Norgaard 2011 (JRME): all seven
  artist-level improvisers described inserting well-learned ideas and
  repeating earlier material. *Supports the lick-extraction premise
  directly.*
- **Deliberate vs explorational.** Johansen 2018 (Psychology of Music):
  jazz students alternate goal-directed drilling with open-ended
  exploration; exact-repetition models are insufficient for improvisation.
  *Qualitative.*
- Spaced repetition / retrieval practice: general evidence only, nothing
  jazz-specific.

## 5. Existing tools

Soundslice (slowdown, looping, transposition), Transcribe! / Amazing Slow
Downer / Anytune (audio loops, 12-key audio cycling), iReal Pro (changes +
backing), Jazz Licks app (fixed library in 12 keys). **None detects devices
in a user's transcription, fragments it, or schedules practice.** That gap
is woodshed's.

## 6. Disagreements

- **Whole solo vs phrases.** Learn Jazz Standards: memorise a full chorus to
  see the form traversed. Jazzadvice / Galper / Reynolds: a few phrases
  learned deeply beat a whole solo learned shallowly. Convergence: learn the
  whole by ear for feel, *practise* in fragments.
- **Paper vs ear.** Ear first, notate afterwards for analysis. A tool that
  takes a `.mxl` sits on the paper side and should push the user back to
  the record.
- **12 keys.** Near-universal among teachers; Bergonzi says a few
  permutations suffice, Galper says only what you like; no study tests
  12-key transfer.

## 7. Implications for woodshed (ranked, with evidence level)

1. **Unit = phrase/device, not whole solo.** Name devices in Coker/Baker
   vocabulary and show where in *this* solo each occurs. Coker, Norgaard,
   Berliner. *Strong.*
2. **"Things to look for" before each exercise** — device, chord function,
   degree numbers. *Moderate (convention).*
3. **Sequence per device:** sing/loop at original pitch → half-step chain →
   drop into the matching slots of a chosen tune → variation prompts (start
   on another beat / chord tone, rhythm-only on one note, reorder fragments)
   → "write 2–3 of your own." Consensus steps 2–9. *Strong as consensus,
   untested as controlled study.*
4. **Interleave, don't block.** Rotate several findings in short blocks and
   mix keys; warn that it feels worse. Stambaugh; Carter & Grahn. *Moderate.*
5. **Rank by recurrence and by the user's taste, not detector score alone.**
   Galper + Duke: surface counts, let the user star items, starred items
   drive the schedule. *Moderate.*
6. **Surface non-pitch features deterministically** — phrase lengths,
   play/rest ratio per chorus, motivic chains (short, local), register.
   Crook's variables; this is the methodology no app does. *Moderate.*
7. **Keep the audio loop in the workflow** — link bar ranges to a
   loop/slowdown player or at least export bar numbers. Every source says
   the record, not the page, is the teacher. *Strong.*
8. Spaced re-tests of earlier findings. *Low (general evidence only).*
